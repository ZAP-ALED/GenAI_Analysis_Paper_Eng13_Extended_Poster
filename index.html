Bias to Burden: A Data Ecofeminist Analysis of GenAI & the Global South

Object of Study — Ana Valdivia's "Data Ecofeminism"

GenAI is not only biased in its outputs but is also built on extractive systems that rely on unequal labor and resource use, with the Global South carrying most of the environmental and social burdens.

01 — Introduction

The Illusion of Immateriality

Generative Artificial Intelligence, also known as GenAI, is often presented as a fast, efficient, and intelligent solution for everyday problems. The technology appears seamless and almost immaterial for its ability to answer questions and generate content. But what many do not see is that this perception hides the complex systems that make these technologies possible.

Behind the convenience of GenAI are massive datasets, energy-intensive facilities, water consumption, material mining, and layers of invisible labor.

Ana Valdivia's "Data Ecofeminism" challenges this illusion by reframing GenAI not only as a technological innovation but as a system deeply embedded in social and environmental inequalities — sustained by extractive practices affecting both people and the planet.

Recent research further supports this view, showing that AI systems depend on interconnected resource systems involving energy, water, materials, and land rather than isolated digital processes (Hlabisa).

02 — Power & Bias

How Power Enters the System

A key argument of Valdivia's framework is that GenAI systems are shaped by existing social, political, and economic power structures rather than operating as neutral tools. Because these systems are trained on vast datasets derived from the internet, they absorb existing social norms, stereotypes, and power relations.

As GenAI learns by identifying statistical patterns, it does not distinguish between neutral information and biased representation. Instead, it treats repeated associations — such as linking women to caregiving roles — as probable truths. Over time, these patterns become embedded into the model itself, allowing it to reproduce and even amplify them in its output.

Bias in GenAI is not accidental — it is a structural feature of how the system is built and trained. Any attempt to "fix" the bias through simple technical adjustments is insufficient, as the problem is rooted in the broader systems that produce and organize data.

The view aligns with broader feminist critiques of knowledge production: data is not objective but socially constructed. Valdivia extends this by arguing that GenAI systems inherit inequalities present in training data — gender, class, and geopolitical hierarchies included.

Valdivia emphasized that the development of GenAI is tied to corporate and patriarchal power. Large technology corporations control the data, infrastructure, and deployment of AI systems, shaping them according to profit-driven priorities. This concentration of power reinforces inequality by privileging certain voices and perspectives while marginalizing others.

GenAI does not only reflect society — it participates in echoing whose knowledge and perspective are prioritized.
— synthesis of Valdivia's Data Ecofeminism framework
03 — Environment

The Material Cost of "Cloud" Technology

A central point of Valdivia is the concept of data ecofeminism, which builds on ecofeminist theory by linking gender inequality with environmental exploitation. This framework challenges the assumption of GenAI being harmlessly "cloud-based" or immaterial. In reality, GenAI depends on vast physical structures such as data centers, servers, and global supply chains.

These infrastructures consume large amounts of water and energy while requiring continuous extraction of raw materials to keep GenAI running. A study by De Vries-Gao showed that AI — especially GenAI — is a major driver of global electricity demand and resource consumption through data centers, challenging the assumption that digital technologies are environmentally neutral.

GenAI systems require freshwater for cooling, electricity generation, and hardware manufacturing. Similarly, D'Orgeval and other researchers argued that GenAI's environmental footprint extends beyond computations to include hardware production and infrastructure development.

Data centers supporting GenAI have been linked to environmental damage costing billions of dollars and increasing pollution in surrounding communities. These impacts show that the environmental costs of GenAI are not minor side effects, but central to its operation.

By emphasizing material impacts, data ecofeminism shifts the discussion from representation to infrastructure. GenAI must be understood not only in terms of what it produces, but also what it consumes — and these costs are distributed through global inequality.

04 — Labor

The Invisible Labor Behind Artificial Intelligence

A crucial aspect of Valdivia's analysis is the uneven distribution of GenAI's costs. While GenAI technologies are largely developed and controlled by companies in the Global North, their environmental and social burdens are disproportionately experienced by the Global South.

The labor behind GenAI is frequently outsourced to workers in the Global South. Research shows that GenAI systems rely on the "invisible labor of data workers," many of whom are located in regions such as Africa and Southeast Asia (Okolo). These workers perform essential tasks such as data labelling, content moderation, and dataset curation — essential to training and maintaining GenAI systems.

Okolo describes these workplaces as "digital sweatshops," where workers endure long hours, low pay, and exposure to disturbing or harmful content with little protection or recognition.

Journalistic investigations by Cheah reveal similar findings: these workers are paid extremely low wages and are exposed to traumatic content for GenAI training, highlighting the human cost hidden behind automated technologies.

The intelligence of GenAI is not purely artificial — it is partially constructed through outsourced human labor that remains deliberately obscured from users.
— analysis of Valdivia's framework & Okolo's research

This invisibility allows companies to market GenAI as efficient and scalable, while concealing the human cost required to sustain it. While the technology is presented as autonomous and intelligent, it is in fact dependent on continuous human intervention. This contradiction is central to understanding GenAI's actual operation.

05 — Colonial Patterns

Digital Colonialism & Global Inequality

The pattern of outsourced and undervalued labor is not new. This practice reflects a longer history of colonial extraction in which wealthier regions benefit from the labor and resources of poorer ones. The inequalities experienced by the Global South are tied to historical patterns of colonialism.

Historical Pattern

Extraction of resources and labor from the Global South for the benefit of more powerful countries in the North, creating persistent economic hierarchies.

Digital Continuation

GenAI continues rather than disrupts existing global hierarchies. The Global South becomes the site where environmental and social costs are displaced, while the Global North captures most of the benefits.

Hlabisa's research further showed that GenAI's ecological footprint is spatially uneven — benefits are concentrated in technologically advanced regions while costs are externalized elsewhere.

Data ecofeminism reveals that GenAI's harms are global but unequal. Together, these findings demonstrate that GenAI depends on a global labor structure in which human work is undervalued, outsourced, and obscured — reinforcing existing inequalities between the Global North and the Global South.

06 — Conclusion

Confronting the Hidden Systems That Sustain GenAI

Valdivia's Data Ecofeminism reveals that GenAI cannot be understood or evaluated as a neutral technological system. This paper has shown that GenAI is shaped by powerful relations rather than neutrality, relying on biased datasets and corporate control. It has demonstrated that these systems rely on material infrastructures that consume significant environmental resources — energy, water, and raw materials. Most importantly, these costs are not evenly distributed.

The Global South bears a disproportionate share of the environmental and social burdens associated with GenAI, which reflects broader patterns of global inequality. Rather than existing as a purely digital or automated innovation, GenAI is embedded within systems of inequality that connect gender bias, environmental exploitation, and global labor dynamics.

A data ecofeminist analysis makes it clear that focusing solely on GenAI outputs is insufficient. The harms of GenAI begin long before a response is generated — found in the data it is trained on, the labor that sustains it, and the resources it consumes. The central question shifts from whether GenAI is biased to how its entire system of production is structured and who ultimately benefits.

If GenAI continues to be developed without addressing these structural inequalities, it risks enforcing the very systems of domination it is often assumed to transcend and go beyond. A more ethical approach to GenAI, therefore, requires not only improving its outputs but also confronting the hidden systems of extraction, labor, and power that sustain it.
