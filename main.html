Bias to Burden:
A Data Ecofeminist Analysis of GenAI and the Global South

Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) is often presented as fast, efficient, and intelligent, appearing almost immaterial in its ability to answer questions and generate content. Yet behind every AI prompt lies a vast material system involving energy-intensive data centers, water consumption, raw material extraction, and invisible labor. This project applies Ana Valdivia’s concept of data ecofeminism to examine how GenAI operates not merely as a neutral technological innovation, but as a system deeply embedded in environmental, labor, and geopolitical inequalities.

Thesis Statement

Generative AI is not only socially biased but materially extractive. Through a data ecofeminist lens, GenAI emerges as a system sustained by unequal labor, environmental exploitation, and corporate power, with the Global South carrying a disproportionate share of its hidden costs.
{/* Analysis Points */}

Key Analysis Points

This analysis shifts the discussion of AI away from outputs alone and toward the infrastructures, labor systems, and unequal global power relations that sustain generative technologies.

⚠️

Bias Is Structural

GenAI systems are trained on massive datasets derived from the internet, absorbing existing social norms, stereotypes, and power relations. Because these systems learn through statistical repetition rather than ethical understanding, they reproduce dominant inequalities involving gender, class, and geopolitical power.

🏭

The “Cloud” Is Physical

The so-called “cloud” depends on physical infrastructures such as data centers, servers, electricity grids, cooling systems, and hardware manufacturing. Research cited in this project shows that GenAI consumes massive amounts of water and energy while relying on continuous extraction of raw materials to sustain its growth.

🌍

Unequal Global Burdens

Although GenAI technologies are largely developed and controlled by corporations in the Global North, much of their environmental damage and invisible labor are displaced onto the Global South. Workers in Africa and Southeast Asia often perform low-paid data labeling and content moderation under exploitative conditions, while poorer regions absorb ecological costs linked to extraction and infrastructure.

Working Synthesis

A data ecofeminist perspective reveals that GenAI cannot be understood solely through the outputs it produces. Its harms begin long before a response is generated, embedded within the data it is trained on, the labor that sustains it, and the environmental resources it consumes. Ethical discussions about AI must therefore confront the hidden systems of extraction, labor, and power that make these technologies possible.
